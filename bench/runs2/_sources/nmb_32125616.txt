Conscious and unconscious brain responses to food and cocaine cues.

Visual presentation of appetitive and negative cues triggers fast responses in the human brain. Here we assessed functional MRI (fMRI) responses to food, cocaine, and neutral cues presented at a subliminal ("unconscious", 33 ms) and supraliminal ("conscious", 750 and 3000 ms) level in healthy, cocaine naïve volunteers. Because there is evidence of circadian variability in reward sensitivity, our second aim was to assess diurnal variability in the brain's reactivity to cues. Sixteen participants completed two randomly ordered fMRI sessions (once 9-11 AM and another 5-7 PM). in which food, cocaine, and neutral cues were presented for 33, 750 and 3000 ms. Participants rated food cues as positive and "wanted" (more so in evenings than mornings), and cocaine cues as negative (no diurnal differences). fMRI showed occipital cortex activation for food>neutral, cocaine>neutral and cocaine>food; dorsolateral prefrontal cortex for cocaine>neutral and cocaine>food, and midbrain for cocaine>food (all p < 0.05). When comparing unconscious (33 ms) > conscious (750 and 3000 ms) presentations, we observed significant differences for cocaine>neutral and cocaine>food in occipital cortex, for cocaine>neutral in the insula/temporal lobe, and for food>neutral in the middle temporal gyrus (p < 0.05). No diurnal differences for brain activations were observed. We interpret these findings to suggest that negative items (e.g., cocaine) might be perceived at a faster speed than positive ones (e.g., food), although we cannot rule out that the higher saliency of cocaine cues, which would be novel to non-drug using individuals, contributed to the faster speed of detection.

Introduction

Brain imaging studies have shown that the visual presentation of food cues trigger responses in brain reward areas, including the ventral striatum and medial prefrontal cortex (Lindgren et al. 2018; Volkow et al. 2008) and in the visual cortices (Wang et al. 2014). Similarly, brain reward regions and visual cortical regions are also active when patients with a substance use disorder view drug-cues (Cabrera et al. 2016; Volkow et al. 2010). Functional MRI (fMRI) cue reactivity paradigms vary in their presentation times: e.g., from a subliminal 33 ms presentation that may lie outside of “conscious” awareness (Childress et al. 2008; Wetherill et al. 2014) to longer time presentations (750-4000 ms) that lead to “conscious” awareness of the cues (Hare et al. 2009; Ihssen et al. 2011; Ludwig et al. 2014; Wiers et al. 2015; Wiers et al. 2014). Although stronger brain activations to subliminal than supraliminal presentations have been reported for phobic stimuli (Siegel et al. 2017), to our knowledge no study has compared the activation patterns elicited by subliminal versus supraliminal presentation of either food or drug cues.

To address this neglect, we assessed the blood oxygenation level dependent (BOLD)-fMRI responses to food, cocaine, and neutral cues presented at different time lengths and compared activation to subliminal presentations versus those that resulted in conscious awareness in healthy, cocaine naïve volunteers. We hypothesized that while subliminal salient cues (food or drugs) would evoke strong responses in limbic and reward regions (amygdala, ventral striatum and ventral frontal cortices), longer presentations would result in greater engagement of cortical regions involved in cognitive control and self-awareness (e.g., dorsolateral prefrontal, medial prefrontal and parietal cortices) (Wetherill et al. 2014). We included two consciously perceived presentation times (i.e., 750 versus 3000 ms) to further test whether the longer presentation time (3000 ms) would result in stronger cortical activation than the shorter one (750 ms). Additionally, because there is evidence of circadian variability in reward sensitivity (Byrne et al. 2017; Webb et al. 2009), we also aimed to assess if the reactivity to food and drug cues show diurnal variation for which we compared cue-induced brain activation in the morning versus evening.

Sixteen healthy volunteers underwent 3 fMRI sessions in which 40 cocaine cues, 40 food cues (20 high-caloric foods, 20 low-caloric foods), and 40 neutral cues were presented in randomized order. In the first session, subjects viewed the cues subliminally for 33 ms, whereas in the second and third sessions, the cues were presented “consciously”; for 750 ms and 3000 ms, respectively. Subjects were tested twice on different days, once in the morning and another time in the evening (in randomized order) to assess diurnal differences in cue reactivity. We hypothesized that the brain activation and behavioral responses to food cues compared to neutral and cocaine cues would be greater in evenings than in mornings, based on findings in rodents of greater activity of the brain reward system in the evening than in the morning (Byrne et al. 2017; Webb et al. 2009). We predicted stronger responses in limbic and reward regions for the food cues, which controls would had been conditioned too, than for cocaine cues, for which they would have not been conditioned since they were naïve to cocaine.

Materials and methods

Participants

Sixteen healthy individuals (6 females and 10 males) with an age range of 21 to 71 years old (M=38.81±13.4SD) and a Body Max Index (BMI) ranging from 17.6 to 34.9 kg/m2 (mean=26.86±5.1SD) completed the study. Participants were recruited and screened to exclude ferromagnetic implants, psychoactive medications and major medical problems, presence of drug abuse or dependence, and neurological or psychiatric disorders (including eating disorders such as binge eating), as assessed by the Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV). Participants were naïve to cocaine use. Participants provided written informed consent according to the Declaration of Helsinki, as part of a task development fMRI study approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Institutes of Health (Combined Neurosciences White Panel).

Food Cue reactivity task

A total of 40 appetitive food, 40 cocaine-related and 40 neutral images were presented in a randomized order in an event-related design using E-prime software. Food pictures included appetitive high and low calorie foods, both savory and sweet, both breakfast and dinner items (i.e., bagels, sandwiches, ice cream and sorbet, salads, fruit salad, pizza, hamburgers, pies). Cocaine pictures included bags of cocaine, lines of cocaine on a table, cocaine in spoons, cocaine pipes, and people smoking cocaine (with faces cut off). Neutral pictures included neutral items from the International Affective Picture System (IAPS) (Lang et al. 2008) and from in-house pictures libraries (Childress et al. 2008; Volkow et al. 2006). Figure 1 shows representative pictures for each category. Picture groups were matched for luminance. Three sessions were developed with different cue presentation times: 33 ms, 750 ms and 3000 ms, and each task consisted of 3 runs showing 40 cues that included all 3 categories of cues (food, cocaine, neutral). Thus, each run showed either 13 or 14 pictures from each picture category to present 40 cues per category equally over 3 runs. The 33 ms paradigm was programmed according to Childress et al. (2008) in which cues were presented for 33 ms, followed by a 467 ms neutral mask (Fig. 1). There was no backward masking for the 750 ms or 3000 ms tasks. We confirmed experimental stimulus duration and stimulus onset/offset -related timing as following: A bright rectangle in the center of the black screen was presented continuously in a loop on and off (black/white) in E-Prime software with the same timing settings used in experiment. A light sensitive diode connected to an oscilloscope was placed in front of the center of the screen. A square waveform was formed in the oscilloscope due to brightness change with a delay of 1.5 ms peak/drop time and a duration accuracy with +-1 ms error. Cues were followed by inter-trial intervals (ITI) of a random number between 4 and 8 s with an average of 6 s, during which a white cross was presented on a black screen. To ensure that participants were paying attention they were instructed to press a button if they saw an animal. Total task durations were 13 min (for the 33 ms presentations of 120 cues), 13:30 min (for the 750 ms presentations of 120 cues) and 18 min (for the 3000 ms presentations of 120 cues).

Each participant performed the 3 sessions twice on 2 separate days, once between 9 and 11 AM and once between 5 and 7 PM, in randomized order. Participants were asked to refrain from eating at least 3 h before the study and were asked to self-report hunger levels before and after the scanning sessions.

After completion of the MRI sessions, participants were asked to rate the cues for their subjective valence (“How negative/positive do you find the picture?”, -3 - 3 [very negative - very positive]); arousal (“How much does the picture excite you?” from 0 to 6 [not at all - extremely]) and wanting (“How much do you want to consume the depicted item right now?” from 0 to 6 [not at all - extremely]), on a 7-point Likert scale.

MRI acquisition and preprocessing

All subjects underwent MRI on a 3 T Magnetom Prisma scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc., Malvern, PA). A 32-channel head coil and a standard echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence were used: sequential interleaved acquisition, repetition time 1.5 s, echo time 30 ms, flip angle α=70°, 64×64 pixels in-plane resolution, 36 slices, slice thickness 4 mm, voxel dimensions 3×3×4 mm3, field of view 192×192 mm2. Stimuli were presented on a black background under dimmed room lighting using a liquid-crystal display screen (BOLDscreen 32, Cambridge Research Systems; UK).

Functional data analysis was performed with SPM8 (Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, UK). During preprocessing, scans were slice-time corrected, spatially realigned, co-registered to the T1 structural images and normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) template. Smoothing was performed with a 6 mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel. Two participants had excessive head motion during their MRI scans (>2 mm), resulting in n=14 participants for further fMRI analyses.

Statistical analysis

We performed repeated measure ANOVAs for valence, arousal and wanting ratings, with two factors: cue type (food, cocaine, neutral) and time of day (AM, PM). Post-hoc t-tests were computed to test whether cues were rated differently than 0 for valence, arousal and wanting.

For every participant and for each session (33, 750 or 3000 ms), 3 fMRI regressors - food, cocaine, and neutral events were created and then convolved with the hemodynamic response function with default temporal filtering of 128 s. Six realignment parameters were included as regressors of no interest. The following contrasts were calculated at the single-subject level: food>neutral, food>cocaine, cocaine>neutral. At the second level, a repeated measures factorial design was used including the first level contrast images with presentation time (33, 750 and 3000 ms) and time of day (am and pm) as within-subject factors.

We performed whole-brain analyses and tested: (1) the main effects of food>neutral, food>cocaine, cocaine>neutral for the three presentation times; (2) the effect of subliminal (33) versus supraliminal (750 and 3000 ms) presentation times; and (3) the effects of morning versus evenings. For whole-brain analyses, we used a combined threshold of p<0.005 uncorrected at the voxel level, with a family-wise error corrected (FWE) threshold of pFWE<0.05 at the cluster level (minimum cluster size k>100). Coordinates are reported in MNI space. We used post-hoc contrasts on the significant clusters from the whole brain analyses to investigate differences between the presentation times. Supplementary Material 1, 2 and 3 provide brain activations for food> neutral, cocaine > neutral and cocaine > food, for each presentation time separately (33, 750, and 3000 ms), at an exploratory threshold of 0.005 uncorrected k>20.

Results

Behavioral ratings

Participants mean self-reported hunger was 3.2±1.7SD on the 7-point Likert scale, with no significant difference between morning versus afternoon (p>0.2).

There was a main effect for cue type for ratings of valence (Roy’s largest root Θ=12.2, F2,14=85.6, p<0.0001) arousal (Θ=4.3, F2,14=30.3, p<0.0001) and wanting (Θ=6.8, F2,14=47.9, p<0.0001) (Fig. 2). Food cues were rated as positive (mean=1.3±0.8SD, t15=6.6, p<.0001), arousing (2.9±1.5SD, t15=8.0, p<0.0001) and wanted (3.2±1.3SD, t15=10.0, p<0.0001), whereas cocaine cues were rated as negative (-2.3±1.1SD, t15=6.6, p<0.0001), not arousing (0.03±0.6SD, t15=2.0, p>0.05) and not wanted (0±0). Neutral cues did not differ from zero on valence (-0.1±0.3SD, t15=1.9, p>0.05) or wanting (0.06±0.1SD, t15=1.9, p>0.05) but were rated as slightly arousing (0.3±0.5SD, t15=2.7, p=0.02).

Main effect of time of day

There were no significant main effects of time of day for any contrasts, and no significant interaction effects between cue type and time of day.

Discussion

Here we compared brain activation patterns for the subliminal versus supraliminal presentation of neutral, food and cocaine cues in healthy volunteers and assessed the effects of two different time durations for the supraliminal presentations. We found a distinct effect of presentation time lengths for the different types of cues. Most notable was that for the cocaine cues compared to neutral and food cues and for the subliminal compared to the supraliminal presentations, we observed greater activation in the visual cortex, dlPFC, and midbrain. In contrast, for both food and neutral cues the supraliminal presentation significantly activated visual cortex whereas the subliminal presentation did not. We discuss these findings in more detail below.

Greater activation for subliminal than supraliminal presentations is reminiscent of findings reported with exposure to phobic stimulation in individuals with a phobic disorder for whom very short versus longer time presentations elicited greater activation in dlPFC, premotor cortex, occipital cortex, superior temporal gyrus and middle frontal gyrus compared to controls (Siegel et al. 2017). A common characteristic of the cocaine cues and the phobic stimuli is that they both have negative valence to the participants; in the current study cocaine cues were evaluated as negative, not arousing or wanted, whereas food cues as positive, arousing and “wanted”, and neutral cues as neutral in these dimensions. We interpret our findings as reflecting stronger subliminal activation by negative compared to positively valenced cues, which suggests that negatively valenced stimuli (cocaine cues, including cocaine paraphernalia and individuals smoking cocaine) might activate the visual cortex at a faster speed than positive stimuli (food cues). This interpretation is supported by psychophysiological studies showing that the brain responds more strongly to negative than positive stimuli, and the differential responses occur extremely quickly (<120 ms) (Smith et al. 2003). Fast preferential responses to negative stimuli are theorized to be part of an adaptive system for quickly detecting a threat (Williams et al. 2006). However, due to a lack of variance in the ratings of cocaine cues (i.e., all participants rated the cocaine cues as negative and non-arousing) we were unable to assess the correlations between the subjective valence ratings and the brain responses to the cocaine cues. Also our healthy volunteers were drug naïve, which might explain why cocaine cues were rated as negative but not arousing, for ratings may have been biased by moral or cultural beliefs. Therefore, our interpretation that negative cues were perceived at a faster speed is preliminary and requires validation. We further showed that the subliminal presentation for cocaine cues activated midbrain more than supraliminal presentations and, although not significant, there was a trend for the subliminal presentation of food to elicit greater midbrain activation than the subliminal presentation. Greater midbrain activation for subliminal presentation of cocaine cues might reflect their greater saliency value (novelty) than food and neutral cues in the cocaine naïve healthy volunteers. Also, although our three picture categories were matched for luminance, some cocaine cues involved people holding cocaine pipes or smoking cocaine and while faces were not included, the representation of humans may have enhanced the saliency of the cocaine cues. Inasmuch as dopamine modulates the reactivity of the occipital cortex to visual stimuli (Zaldivar et al. 2018) enhanced saliency of cocaine cues could have driven their strong activation from subliminal presentation.

We hypothesized strong responsivity to subliminal food cues in brain reward regions (e.g., midbrain, ventral striatum), and that longer food presentations would result in greater engagement of cortical regions involved with conscious awareness (i.e., PFC, parietal cortex) (Lindgren et al. 2018; Wetherill et al. 2014). For the food > neutral 33 ms presentation time only, we found activation in midbrain and thalamus (Supplementary Fig. 1A), whereas the mPFC, ACC, dlPFC, and insula showed activations for the 3000 ms condition only (Supplementary Fig. 1C), partly supporting our hypothesis. Because of strong deactivations of the middle temporal gyrus (BA 37) in the 750 and 3000 ms conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1B and 1C), however, the main effect of conscious versus unconscious presentation times appeared as an activation in the bilateral middle temporal gyri (Fig. 4B). Moreover, when collapsing the 3 presentation times together, only the occipital cortex showed activation for food > neutral, indicating increased visual attention for food cues over all presentation times (Fig. 3A). Consistent with prior studies that reported that masked phobic stimuli activated the amygdala (Carlsson et al. 2004; Larson et al. 2006; Lipka et al. 2011), we also observed that cocaine cues activated the amygdala when subliminally presented compared to both food and neutral cues, but not for the supraliminal presentations (See Supplemental Table 2 and 3).

Post-hoc comparisons of main effects for cocaine > neutral and cocaine > food in both occipital cortex and dlPFC (and for cocaine > food also in midbrain) showed stronger activations in 750 ms than 3000 ms (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table 2 and 3). This finding is in contrast to our hypothesis that longer supraliminal stimulations would result in stronger cortical activation (3000 ms) than shorter supraliminal presentation times (750 ms). However, at an uncorrected level, Supplemental Figs. 1 and 2 show that for the presentation of both cocaine and food cues contrasted with neutral, the 3000 ms elicited greater activation in medial prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex and insula than the 750 ms. Thus, this may have implications for the design of brain activation experiments, as these results suggest that longer presentation times are more likely to recruit medial prefrontal and insula regions than shorter ones. Future studies with different stimuli are needed to further explore stimulus durations.

Our second aim was to assess diurnal variability in brain reactivity to cues by comparing responses in the morning versus those in the evenings. Previous studies have shown diurnal variation in food- and drug-seeking behavior, with a preference of drug and food intake in the evening regulated by mPFC and NAc (Baltazar et al. 2013; Webb et al. 2009). We found that food cues were rated as more “wanted” in evenings (5-7 PM) than in mornings (9-11 AM), which is partly in line with a previous study that found diurnal variations in “wanting” (Byrne and Murray 2017). However, Byrne and Murray (2017) found a peak in “wanting” at 2 PM (compared to 10 AM and 7 PM), and suggested, along with others (Boivin et al. 1997; Murray et al. 2009), that self-reported positive affect may peak in early afternoon rather than late afternoon. We hypothesized that activations in brain reward areas would follow the diurnal rhythm in self-reports of wanting., i.e. greater striatal activations in PM than AM. However, we did not find evidence for diurnal effects on brain responses to food, or cocaine in either direction. A recent neuroimaging study found evidence for higher neural reward activation at times of day when rewards are unexpected; i.e., greater putamen activation to reward at 10 AM and 7 PM compared to 2 PM (Byrne et al. 2017). Therefore, the lack of effect we found in our current study may be due to the times we picked (i.e, 9-11 AM and 5-7 PM) and future studies should include an early afternoon assessment (e.g., 2 PM). Alternatively, it is possible that despite being rated as “wanted”, the food cues do not elicit the same type of reward response as the monetary reward cues that were used by Byrne et al. (2017).

A limitation to the study is that we did not ask participants whether cues were perceived consciously or unconsciously in the three presentation times. Since our task was identical to that performed by Childress et al. (2008) who found that 33 ms cues were not consciously recollected, we argue that our cue perception likely shows similar characteristics. Another limitation is that we tested food cues and cocaine cues that were positively and negatively valenced respectively and based on the results we argue that negatively valenced cues trigger brain responses faster (e.g. at 33 ms) than positively valenced ones. However, whether this is the case for more general positive and negative cues (e.g., tender versus threatening/disgusting stimuli) remains to be investigated. In addition, our study only included healthy participants and individuals with a cocaine use disorder would likely show different responses to cocaine cues, and likewise, obese individuals might have yielded different responses to food cues, as prior studies would suggest (Castellanos et al. 2009; Jastreboff et al. 2013; Wilcox et al. 2011). Additionally, food cues might have generated greater activation for the subliminal presentation had we tested participants after a longer period of fasting. Because of the limited sample size, we were unable to assess whether we could corroborate prior findings of gender differences in reactivity to food (Frank et al. 2010) and cocaine cues (Volkow et al. 2011). The BMI of our participants had a wide range (17.6 to 34.9), indicative of underweight, normal weight and obese and while it is representative of the general population, future studies with a homogenous BMI group are needed in order to replicate our findings. Last, some of the cocaine cues but not the neutral or food cues involved people holding cocaine pipes or smoking cocaine and although faces were not included, the representation of humans may have driven some of our main findings for cocaine cues.

In sum, we found strong activation of visual cortex, insula/temporal lobe and midbrain to subliminally presented negatively rated cocaine cues in healthy volunteers, which we interpret to indicate that negative stimuli may trigger earlier awareness than positive or neutral stimuli.
