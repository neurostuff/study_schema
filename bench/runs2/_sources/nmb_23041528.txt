# Similar neural mechanisms for perceptual guesses and free decisions

DOI: 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2012.09.064
PII: S1053811912009822

## Keywords

Free decisions
Guessing
Precuneus
Functional MRI
Multivariate pattern classification

## Abstract

When facing perceptual choices under challenging conditions we might believe to be purely guessing. But which brain regions determine the outcome of our guesses? One possibility is that higher-level, domain-general brain regions might help break the symmetry between equal-appearing choices. Here we directly investigated whether perceptual guesses share brain networks with other types of free decisions. We trained an fMRI-based pattern classifier to distinguish between two perceptual guesses and tested whether it was able to predict the outcome of similar non-perceptual choices, as in conventional free choice tasks. Activation patterns in the medial posterior parietal cortex cross-predicted free decisions from perceptual guesses and vice versa. This inter-changeability strongly speaks for a similar neural code for both types of decisions. The posterior parietal cortex might be part of a domain-general system that helps resolve decision conflicts when no choice option is more or less likely or valuable, thus preventing behavioural stalemate.

## Introduction

Perceptual decision-making is essential for our every-day life and seems to happen effortlessly when sufficient sensory information is available. Perceptual decision-making under varying sensory conditions has been intensively studied using single-cell recordings in monkeys ( ) and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG) in humans ( ). One challenging question, however, is how we make decisions in the absence of sufficient information when stimuli are very difficult to see. This problem is exacerbated by the absence of “priors” in many perceptual tasks, where choice alternatives are equally likely and rewarding. Participants are thus frequently faced with a ‘symmetry situation’: How do we decide between equal options without getting stuck like Buridan's mythical donkey, starving in the middle between two haystacks? One possibility is that we rely on the same sensory neural systems as for veridical perception but that random noise fluctuations in the sensory system determine trial-by-trial choices ( ). Another possibility is that domain-general regions are used to break the symmetry between equal options ( ).

Using fMRI in combination with pattern classification techniques ( ) we recently assessed which brain regions predict perceptual choices under high and low visibility. We found that under low visibility, perceptual choices about objects could be decoded from medial posterior parietal cortex, but not from sensory brain regions ( ). This parietal region was clearly distinct from sensory areas in the lateral-occipital complex (LOC) in ventral visual cortex, which encoded sensory information about object categories ( ). The medial parietal area strongly overlapped with a network that was recently found to be involved in free decision-making ( ). Thus, we reasoned that guessing and free decisions might share similar brain networks, a hypothesis that is yet to be tested.

Here, we conducted an event-related fMRI study in which participants made category choices about pianos and chairs using three different conditions. In all conditions participants saw brief, repeated mask–target–mask sequences. (1) Perceptual choices under high visibility: On each trial the sequence contained an object image (piano or chair) that was clearly visible. Participants were asked to identify the category of the presented object. (2) Perceptual choices under “low visibility” (perceptual guesses): Participants were given the same task but the timing of the stimuli was chosen to yield strong masking and the sequence only contained a neutral noise image instead of an object. The participants were unaware of this manipulation (as confirmed with post-experimental questionnaires and interviews) and thus believed to be making normal (albeit difficult) perceptual guesses. Thus, this condition was in fact a zero-visibility condition. (3) Free choices: The presentation was identical to the “low visibility” condition, again ensuring that no visual bias could drive the decisions. Participants were asked to spontaneously and freely choose either “piano” or “chair”, whatever category came first to their minds. They were instructed to ignore the visual stimulus display (even though it did not contain any differential visual information). We then used a “searchlight” decoding approach ( ) to search for brain regions that encoded participants' category choices in all three conditions. In order to identify potential neural representations of the choices, we identified regions that exhibit different response patterns to the different choice options. This classification-based approach is frequently used in order to identify information-coding neural response patterns. The crucial analysis used activation patterns associated with decision outcomes from perceptual guessing to cross-predict free decisions, and vice versa. This analysis aimed to search for any brain region in which the choice-predictive activation patterns were inter-changeable between the two conditions. Finding such a brain region would not provide conclusive evidence for a shared neural code for both decisions, as also non-decision related processes could systematically differ between the choice options. It would be a strong indicator, however, that such pattern similarity may reflect important similarities in decision-making.

## Material and methods

### Participants

Sixteen right-handed participants with normal or corrected to normal visual acuity gave written informed consent and participated in the study. The experiment was approved by the local ethics committee and was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. One participant indicated that he had noticed the missing object images and was excluded. The data from the remaining 15 participants (7 female; mean age 25 years; range 21–28) were used for the analyses.

### Stimuli and experimental procedure

The stimuli were 24 pictures of pianos and chairs, created from freely available pictures from the Internet or custom-made photographs, showing objects in different natural backgrounds ( ). Pictures were transformed into grey-scale versions with a size of 400 × 400 pixels and were presented on a grey background. Two different scrambled masks (pre- and post-mask) were used during presentation. Masks consisted of 10 × 10 squares from random object pictures, each 40 × 40 pixels, rearranged such that they did not contain any identifiable parts of objects. The visual angle was 7.15° (stimuli and masks). For the invisibility condition, one noise image was used that consisted of scrambled phase textures. To create this, a two-dimensional Fourier transformation was performed on one of the masks. The phase map was scrambled by adding a random value of +/− 1.75*Π to each phase angle ( ). The resulting phase maps were then transformed back to an image and contrast-normalized. Note that all noise trials used the same noise stimulus to avoid biasing participants' choices by incidental variations in similarity to an object category.

Each trial consisted of a brief “standing wave” of the same mask–target–mask sequence ( ) repeated three times, with a white fixation cross centrally superimposed. A combination of forward- and backward-masking was applied. For the high visibility condition, on each trial one object image was shown, preceded by a 150 ms pre-mask, then presented for 66.7 ms, and followed by a post-mask (500 ms minus target duration = 433.3 ms). For the perceptual guessing condition, only the noise image was shown, also preceded by a 150 ms pre-mask, then presented for 16.7 ms, and followed by a post-mask (500 ms minus target duration = 483.3 ms) ( ).

The experiment implemented three conditions (equal number of trials per condition in each run). The first two conditions required the participants to categorize either the highly visible objects (high visibility condition, HVC) or objects they believed to be poorly visible (perceptual guessing condition, PGC). The third condition was a free decision condition (FDC) for the same two categories. No feedback was given during the experiment. The task was indicated by the colour of the fixation cross in the beginning of each trial (red = categorization; green = free decision; duration 750 ms). For the HVC, on each trial one object stimulus (piano or chair) was shown. For the PGC participants were told that an object stimulus was presented, albeit that it was sometimes difficult to detect. However, only the strongly masked noise image was presented which created the strong impression that there was indeed an object presented without revealing its identity. For the FDC, participants were presented with the same stimulus sequence as for the PGC but they were instructed to spontaneously choose the category that first came to their mind, independent of the presentation. It was pointed out that they should choose an object category instead of a response button and not change their mind after making a decision. Participants were also asked to always make a decision, even if uncertain. The use of noise stimuli guaranteed that participants' decisions were pure guesses or free choices (for pianos or chairs), without residual visibility influencing either process. Participants indicated their choice by pressing one of two response buttons (right index- and middle finger), pseudo-randomized presented on either side of the fixation cross (2000 ms). This manipulation allowed the de-correlation of motor responses and category choices. It was followed by a jittered fixation period (7 s, 8 s or 9 s). Participants performed six runs (72 trials per run). The same object image was never shown twice in one run. The order of object images was individually randomized for each participant and each run. Adherence to the task instructions and success of the experimental manipulation were assessed after the experiment using a questionnaire (for results see ).

### fMRI data acquisition

Functional MRI volumes of the whole brain were acquired using a Siemens TRIO 3 T scanner (Erlangen, Germany) with a standard head coil (42 axial slices, TR = 2800 ms, echo time TE = 30 ms, resolution 3 × 3 × 2 mm 3 with 1 mm gap). Within each of the six runs, 208 volumes were acquired for each participant using gradient-echo EPI. The first two volumes of every scan were discarded by default to allow for magnetic saturation effects.

### fMRI data analyses

First, we performed pattern classification analyses ( ) in order to identify brain regions encoding information about category choices within each decision condition separately. Because the PGC and FDC were unconstrained by the stimulus and participants were free to make either choice, the data of 8 runs had to be excluded from the analyses for these conditions because they were too unbalanced (< 4 trials per condition). One participant's choices were strongly imbalanced in more than half of the runs and were completely excluded. Another participant's data was excluded from the analyses for the HVC because of bad categorization performance for chairs (< 70%). Note that by excluding unbalanced runs, we avoided biasing the subsequent analyses ( ).

First, motion corrected and unsmoothed brain images were used for a general linear model (GLM), which was then used for statistical analyses on the individual participant level (using SPM2, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/ ). Trials were assigned to piano- and chair-trials according to participants' choices in all three conditions (HVC, PGC, FDC) in separate analyses. For each run, trials were modelled as events, beginning with the presentation of the cue, and convolved with the canonical haemodynamic response function (HRF), to obtain the regressors. Note that it was impossible to control for the exact time-point of trial-by-trial choices. It might be possible that free decisions might have been made slightly earlier than guesses (in post-experimental questionnaires participants indicated having made their free decisions either during the cue period or during the object period). The cue period, however, lasted only 750 ms and our model captured both the cue- and the object period (which could not be de-correlated given that the TR was 2800 ms). Thus, the decision period was always included for all conditions. The parameter estimates for each voxel position formed the basis of subsequent searchlight decoding analyses for choices in all three conditions. This method allows the search for information encoding regions in the brain and is thus unbiased in terms of a-priori anatomical hypotheses (for details see ).

For a given position in the brain, a searchlight cluster was defined as a sphere of N voxels (c 1…N ), with radius of 4 voxels ( ), constructed around the central voxel v i . The GLM parameter estimates were extracted for these searchlight voxels for each category and run, separately for each participant and for each condition. A multivariate pattern classification algorithm was applied to decode the choice category from these pattern vectors. For this, we used a linear support vector pattern classifier with a fixed regularisation parameter C = 1 that was trained on pattern vectors from all runs but one (“training data set”). Based on the training data, the classifier was used to predict the categories from the pattern vectors of an independent “test data set” taken from the remaining run (LIBSVM toolbox, http://www.csie.ntu.edu.tw/~cjlin/libsvm ). A six-fold cross-validation was performed by repeating the classification process independently with the pattern vectors of each run as the “test data set”. This procedure avoids problems of circular inference. The use of the same number of exemplars for decoding for each decision and each run guaranteed that both decision outcomes were always equally represented in the training data. The analyses for each decision condition were independent of the other analyses and testing and training were strictly based on activation patterns from the same decision condition.

The mean probability of correct classification (decoding accuracy) assigned to each central voxel of a searchlight was then combined to three-dimensional maps of local decoding accuracy values for each condition and each participant. These maps were normalized to a standard stereotaxic space (Montreal Neurological Institute EPI template) and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm FWHM. Random-effects group-level analyses were computed on a voxel-by-voxel basis to identify searchlight positions where local patterns had significant decoding accuracy across all participants ( ).

We performed additional control decoding analyses for stimuli in the HVC and motor responses across all conditions ( ). Using randomized response mappings made it possible to dissociate between categorical choices and motor responses. The classification procedure was the same as described above, only that parameter estimates were based on a GLM modelling the stimuli or the motor responses, respectively.

Our main analysis was a cross-condition classification analysis , which was performed for the PGC and the FDC. The pattern vectors from one condition (e.g. perceptual guessing) were used to train the classifier to distinguish between choices for pianos and chairs, but instead of predicting the participants' choices in the one remaining run within the same condition, it was used to predict decisions from the left-out run for the other condition (e.g. free decisions, and vice versa). This procedure maximised the comparability with the previous analyses (e.g. the number of training and test data sets and cross-validation steps) ( ), but modifications (e.g. training on all runs from one condition) did not change the results. Both ways of cross-condition classification were performed and an ANOVA was applied followed by an inclusive conjunction test for both contrasts. Only regions that successfully supported both directions of prediction were considered significant. Cross-condition analyses between other combinations of conditions did not reveal significant results and are not reported.

We further ran additional analyses to confirm that our results were not biased by individually unbalanced decisions. For this, participants were divided into two groups with respect to their decision balance. Groups (BI high mean = 26.19%; SD ± 8.45; BI low mean = 9.23%; SD ± 4.41) were divided by median split (BI median = 10%). The absolute difference in percentage of decisions (averaged across PGC and FDC) was taken as a balance indicator (BI): BI = (| pianos (%) − chairs (%) |). Two-tailed T -tests for independent samples were conducted to test whether these groups differed in decoding accuracies for i) average searchlight decoding results for the whole brain volume (pointing to general methodological problems caused by decision imbalance) or ii) decoding results for the best searchlight clusters (pointing to specific regional biases caused by decision imbalance).

### Univariate control analyses

Conventional univariate analyses were conducted on the fMRI data to investigate whether any areas showed differences in general activation when participants were presented with (or chose) pianos compared to chairs. Additionally, areas should be identified that differed in activation for the high visibility condition, the guessing condition and the free decision condition. First, all data was motion corrected to the first volume of the first run using SPM2. All volumes were then normalized to MNI space, re-sampled to a voxel size of 3 × 3 × 3 mm 3 and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm FWHM. A GLM was used for statistical analyses on the participant level. Modelling was the same as described for the multivariate analyses, resulting in separate regressors for piano and chair trials, individually for each experimental condition and each run. Contrasts between pianos and chairs were calculated for each condition. The three experimental conditions were contrasted across object categories. Results are reported in . reports the activation and deactivation profiles for the whole brain in the univariate control analysis. The specific encoding of decision outcomes, however, could only be addressed using multivariate pattern classification.

## Results

### High visibility condition

The average hit rate for both visible pianos (85.2%) and chairs (91.5%) was very high, indicating that the sensory information was used to make decisions ( ). Second, the searchlight classification analysis revealed that the category of highly visible object stimuli was encoded in the bilateral visual cortex/LOC (accuracy 61%; ). Third, replicating our earlier findings ( ), choices under high visibility could exclusively be decoded from the LOC ( p < .05, FWE-corrected for clusters, 10 voxel cluster threshold; accuracy left LOC 58%; accuracy right LOC 61%; ). Note that under high visibility choices and stimuli are highly correlated and can thus not be dissociated, but the high visibility condition was not the key focus of the present experiment. It was mainly included to keep participants engaged during the perceptual decision making tasks.

### Perceptual guessing condition

Participants chose pianos in 47.5% and chairs in 51.2% of all trials (1.3% trials were missed), pointing towards overall balanced selections ( t (13) = 0.93; p = .37; ). Please note that in this condition choices were independent from the neutral stimuli. We found that the choice outcome of perceptual guesses could only be decoded from the medial posterior parietal cortex/precuneus ( p < .05, FWE-corrected for clusters; 10 voxel cluster threshold; accuracy 61%; ), thus replicating findings from our previous study with stimuli that could be recognized just above chance ( ). This cluster extended laterally to the inferior parietal cortex and medially to the posterior cingulate cortex, a region previously found in free choice tasks ( ).

### Perceptual guesses and free choices

We then investigated whether the region in the medial posterior parietal cortex contained a similar representation for guesses and free decisions. We found that two clusters bilaterally located in the precuneus allowed a bi-directional classification such that guesses could be predicted from classifiers trained on free choices and vice versa ( p < .05, FWE-corrected for clusters; 10 voxel cluster threshold; peak accuracy 57%; ). This network substantially overlapped with the recently identified free decision network ( ) and the region that we found to be involved in perceptual guessing ( ). This finding supports our hypothesis of a similar neural code for guesses and free decisions in the medial posterior parietal cortex.

Note that also for free choices, the two selections (pianos, chairs) were balanced in our sample with 51.5% for pianos and 47.5% for chairs ( t (13) = 0.90; p = .38; 1.0% trials were missed; ). Searchlight decoding showed that besides the medial parietal cortex ( p = .009 uncorrected; accuracy 57%; see ), outcomes of free decisions were also encoded in the anterior medial prefrontal cortex (aMPFC; p < .05, FWE-corrected for clusters; 10 voxel cluster threshold; accuracy 64%; ). Importantly, unlike the medial parietal regions, the anterior prefrontal regions did not encode guesses (accuracy 48%, p > .1 uncorrected; contrast FDC > PGC, p < .00001, uncorrected) and did not allow for cross-prediction of guesses and free choices (both directions 53%, p > .01 uncorrected).

### Control analyses

The precuneus has been discussed to be part of the brain's default mode network ( ). In our study, the precuneus was activated for all conditions ( ). Crucially, we did not observe activation differences between pianos and chair choices for any condition (all p > .1). Deactivations during the task period were observed in the medial prefrontal cortex for all conditions ( D). However, the deactivated cluster for free decisions (MNI [− 3 21 − 6]) was more dorsal compared to the cluster that encoded free decisions (MNI [3 48 21]) and did not overlap.

The additional analysis that used median split to divide participants in two groups of more balanced (BI high ) or less balanced (BI low ) showed that there were no significant differences in the overall decoding accuracies between the two groups, neither for the PGC (BI high 51.7%, SD ± 2.45; BI low 49.6%, SD ± 2.71; T (6) = 1.41; p = 0.21) nor for the FDC (BI high 51.2%, SD ± 2.24; BI low 50.6%, SD ± 2.40; T (6) = 0.44; p = 0.68). There were also no significant differences in decoding accuracies for the specific searchlight clusters for guessing decisions in a) precuneus/PCC (MNI [24 − 45 18]; BI high 60.5%, SD ± 12.60; BI low 57.6%, SD ± 4.29; T (6) = 0.60; p = 0.57) and b) precuneus/PPC (MNI [30 − 63 51]; BI high 63.2%, SD ± 10.37; BI low 58.1%, SD ± 10.15; T (6) = 1.29; p = 0.25). Again, no differences could be found for decoding accuracies for free decisions in MPFC (MNI [3 48 21]; BI high 61.3%, SD ± 9.21; BI low 65.9%, SD ± 8.15; T (6) = − 1.06; p = 0.33). The same picture emerged when BIs were calculated for each condition (PGC, FDC) separately. It can therefore be concluded that differences in decision ratios cannot explain the pattern classification results.

Evidence that participants did not confuse the perceptual condition and the free decision condition comes from our detailed questionnaires. First, participants reported with great consensus having made free decisions without confusions (93.3% responded with always or mostly, 6.7% sometimes, 0% rarely or never; see A). Second, they indicated predominantly having made free decisions early in the trial whilst perceptual guesses could only be made after the stimulus period (for details see A). Additionally, response time data confirmed that free decision responses were given significantly faster ( p = .0016) than responses for perceptual guessing, providing evidence that participants could distinguish well between tasks ( B). Furthermore, univariate control analyses show differences in activation in several brain regions between the two tasks (see and for details). There is also some evidence for attentional modulation effects in visual areas. A region of interest (ROI) analysis of the occipital areas (as defined in the MarsBar Toolbox for SPM; ) showed that the middle occipital ROI was significantly more strongly activated for the HVC compared to the FDC (left: T = 1.85, p = .03; right: T = 2.14, p = .02) and by trend more strongly activated for the PGC compared to the FDC (left: T = 1.23; p = 0.11; right: T = 1.26, p = .10). Thus, when no attention to the stimuli was required during free decisions, activation was reduced in some visual areas. Taken together, these results support the assumption that participants did not confuse the decision tasks.

Finally, control analyses confirmed that motor responses were only encoded in the left motor cortex (accuracy 58%; ) and not in choice-related regions, confirming the successful de-correlation of motor responses and choice categories.

## Discussion

In summary, we demonstrated that a region in the precuneus, extending laterally to the inferior parietal cortex and medially to the posterior cingulate cortex, encoded guesses when participants were required to make perceptual decisions about invisible objects. Supporting our a priori hypothesis, we showed that activation patterns for guesses and free decisions in these regions allowed for the prediction of outcomes from one type of decision from activation patterns associated with the other. One interpretation of this finding is that under certain conditions perceptual guesses and free decisions might be partly based on the same neural mechanisms.

Recent studies using fMRI have made great progress in shedding light on human perceptual decision-making ( ). The sensory regions were found to be strongly activated with increasing visibility ( ), pointing to a role of these regions in processing the decision-relevant sensory information. This is in line with our finding of an increased encoding of sensory stimuli and choices under high visibility. However, most of the abovementioned fMRI studies either did not explicitly investigate choice prediction ( ), did not include zero visibility conditions ( ) and none of them investigated free choices.

Some fMRI and EEG studies also used pattern classification techniques to predict perceptual choices from brain signals ( ). However, these studies either did not implement a pure noise condition that required perceptual guessing, or focussed on a priori defined regions of interests, mostly in the visual cortex. Others have shown that stimulus information in LOC depends on successful perception ( ). However, this pertained to the encoding of stimuli under low visibility, not to the encoding of choices . Note that stimuli and choices are fully de-correlated when performance is at chance. Thus, these studies also did not unravel which mechanism underlies perceptual guesses. Most importantly, these studies did not compare perceptual guessing with free choices, which was the key aim of the present study.

In the present study we demonstrated the involvement of regions in the medial parietal cortex in perceptual guessing, replicating our previous findings ( ). These regions were mainly located in the mid-precuneus, a region involved in self-related processing, episodic memory and experience of agency ( ), as well as in the posterior cingulate cortex, which was found to be involved in decision making and flexibly adapting behaviour under uncertainty ( ). Most importantly, our study provided first evidence that this parietal network is directly linked to different internally generated decisions – perceptual guessing and free decisions – by cross-predicting choices from the respective associated local spatial activation patterns. In both cases participants could not use external cues to guide their decisions. Thus, one interpretation of our data is that the similarity between activation patterns associated with decision outcomes in both decision tasks reflects a shared neural mechanism for internal decisions. Note that the decoding accuracy is based on the group average of all individual subject accuracy maps and therefore provides a conservative estimate. Our results, however, cannot be interpreted as evidence that the underlying neural code was identical. There is an ongoing debate whether such internal decisions may still be influenced by hidden internal cues, such as previous actions, preferences or goals (see ). Our study was not designed to resolve this question; however it clearly demonstrates a strong similarity of predictive neural patterns in the posterior parietal cortex in two different internally guided decision conditions. In line with our results, the posterior parietal areas were found to be involved in the representation of generic categorical associations ( ). It has to be noted, however, that our result could also be due to other systematic differences between the object conditions than the decision itself. For example, strong and category-specific motor imagery or attentional processes could accompany the decisions and lead to the observed similarities of activation patterns. The cross-classification result itself cannot unravel the underlying mental process as it only reflects a reliable difference between activation patterns for both categories across conditions. This means since we cannot know which other processes might additionally systematically differ between decision outcomes, the conclusion that it reflects the decision process has to be made with care. However, our paradigm required participants to make decisions between objects on each trial. This makes it likely that our results indeed reflect decision-related processes. It has also been suggested based on theoretical considerations that whenever there is an equal amount of evidence for choices options (as in guesses) or no choice option is more or less valuable than the other (as in free decisions), noise fluctuations in a central, domain-general system ( ) might drive the decision outcome and break the resulting symmetry. This could be one explanation for the encoding of domain-general, self-related choices for symmetry situations in the posterior parietal cortex, as observed here and in earlier studies ( ).

Other sub-regions of the parietal cortex have been shown to be sensitive to object information ( ). In contrast, the mid-parietal region found in our study was not sensitive to sensory information, but to the specific choices . In that respect our mid-parietal region is more similar to regions of the intraparietal sulcus (IPS) found to be choice-predictive in monkey studies on coherent motion discrimination ( ). However, in our study the mid-parietal region was only choice predictive under low visibility conditions. It is unlikely that our choice decoding for perceptual guessing and free decision-making directly reflects a diffusion process of the decision variable ( ) towards a decision threshold in the parietal cortex. The reason is that the integral of an accumulated evidence signal (which would be the best predictor of the fMRI signal) would be highest for high visibility, not for low visibility conditions (see Fig. 7 in ).

Importantly, we can exclude that we confounded choices and specific motor planning because motor responses and choices were de-correlated by using pseudo-randomized response mapping screens. This approach achieved a cognitive orthogonalisation of both processes. This means, within each experimental condition, the same choice option was equally often mapped to each response button. The only systematic difference between the activation patterns used for decision decoding was the category choice made. Thus, in our study the medial parietal cortex encoded choices, not motor responses. As our mass-univariate analyses show, the parietal regions were further involved in the task, including motor preparation; however, we could not decode the specific motor responses from anywhere but the motor cortex. Another alternative hypothesis is that participants used mental imagery during guessing and that the precuneus might have been involved in creating images from memory ( ). However, mental imagery should mainly involve the same areas as used for veridical judgements ( ). This is supported by several pattern classification studies, which demonstrated the encoding of viewed objects as well as imagined objects in the ventral visual cortex but not parietal cortex ( ). Thus, at this stage a plausible explanation for our findings is that the precuneus might be generally involved in internal decision-making.

Our results suggest that participants did not use residual signals in the sensory cortex to break the symmetry between different choice options under very low visibility. We cannot exclude that the classifier is missing content-selective information in the visual cortex under the guessing condition. However, we deliberately provided no discriminative information in this condition and participants had to guess. This does not rule out that for other stimuli and tasks participants might continue extracting apparent stimulus information from pure noise signals, as suggested for example by work on classification images ( ) or by studies investigating decisions about motion direction in random dot kinematograms in monkeys ( ). Gradually manipulating discriminative information may also facilitate the set-up of a perceptual threshold, which might lead to different results. In line with this, populations of neurons in the parietal cortex have been shown to change their choice-related coding depending on trial-by-trial confidence or the possibility to opt out of a decision ( ). This means, if the task instructions or the feedback encouraged participants to entirely rely on the perceptual stimulus information, it could be speculated that a stronger similarity of activation patterns in the visual areas may allow cross-condition prediction of choices between high and extremely low visibility conditions. Disentangling the exact relationship between feedback, motivation and strategies in perceptual guessing needs to be addressed by future research. Future studies may also test whether the parietal cortex is causally involved in the decision process, for example by applying transcranial magnetic stimulation to disrupt its functions. More research is needed to confirm our interpretation that the high similarity in representations of perceptual guesses and free decisions in the precuneus justifies the assumption of a shared neural mechanism.

An additional result was that whilst activation patterns in the posterior parietal cortex were inter-changeable between guesses and free decisions, activation patterns in the anterior medial PFC were not. This area uniquely encoded free decisions, as demonstrated previously ( ) and it has been suggested to represent the most abstract contents of cognition ( ). Only when participants intended to make free decisions in our study, could the decision outcomes be predicted from the anterior medial PFC. Neurons in these regions could be tuned to different decision outcomes, whilst having the capacity to be flexibly re-coded depending on task demands. This is in line with a theory previously suggested for the prefrontal cortex ( ). Thus, intended free decision-making might be achieved by the medial prefrontal and posterior parietal regions working together in concert. The difference between perceptual guessing and free decisions might be a difference in the intention to decide freely or internally. Perceptual guesses and free choices could be regarded as “intended internal choices” and “unintended internal choices”, respectively.

## Conclusion

Our study is the first to demonstrate that perceptual guesses can be predicted from neural patterns for free decisions and vice versa. Whilst it is not possible to interpret the similarity in fine-grained activation patterns as unambiguously and directly representing the neural code of the decisions, the most likely interpretation of our results is that it points towards a common mechanism for internal decisions when external input cannot be used to resolve a decision conflict. Thus, we provided the first evidence for a domain-general mechanism, which might be involved in overcoming symmetric decision situations, as occurring during perceptual guessing as well as during free decision-making.
